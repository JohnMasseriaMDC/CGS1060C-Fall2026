How the simulations work

04SimNet: Show Me, Guide Me, Let Me Try

When you open a SimNet lesson, you get what looks like a live copy of Windows or Microsoft Word running in your browser. The lesson gives you a task — "pin Word to the Start menu," "display the open Word window from the taskbar" — and you actually perform it. A single lesson may contain dozens of these tasks; the Windows 11 lesson we looked at had 44.

Each task offers three modes:

ModeWhat it doesEarns credit?
Show MePlays a narrated video demonstrating the task start to finish.No
Guide MeWalks you through it step by step, telling you exactly what to click and confirming each click.No
Let Me TryGives you the task and nothing else. You do it yourself.Yes
Strategy

Try Let Me Try first. If you get it wrong you can retry, and there is no penalty for attempts. Only if you are genuinely stuck should you fall back to Guide Me to learn the steps, then come back and do it in Let Me Try so it counts.

You only have to complete each task one way, and finishing it checks the item off the list. You get credit for the lesson when all of its tasks are complete — all 44 of them, in the Windows 11 example. Exit the lesson properly when you are done so your progress records.

Policies

07Artificial intelligence in this course

AI is part of the curriculum here, not something we pretend does not exist. Part of what I want to teach you is how these tools work and how to use them well. So the policy is not "no AI." It is about where in your process the AI shows up.

Encouraged

Asking an AI to explain a concept, define a term, show you what a feature is capable of, or check your understanding after you have attempted the work yourself. "How does conditional formatting work in Excel?" is a great question to ask an AI.

Not permitted

Copying an assignment prompt or question into an AI tool and submitting what it gives back. That is plagiarism, and it earns a zero for the assignment.

The reasoning is practical rather than moral. Learning a skill works like learning an instrument — you get good by doing it badly, repeatedly, until you do not. Pasting the problem into a chatbot skips the part where the learning happens, and you leave the course with a grade and no ability. When you get stuck on a question, the better move is to use the online textbook to find the answer in the text, or to use Circle In, which is designed to guide rather than to solve.

Content

08Concepts introduced

These came up in discussion rather than from a chapter. All of them return later in the course.

Recursion

When a screen share captures itself, you see a display inside a display inside a display, apparently forever. In programming, a function that calls itself is recursive. It is a legitimate and useful technique, but a recursive function needs a condition that stops it. Without one you get infinite recursion, and the program eventually exhausts the computer's memory and crashes.

Binary and punched cards

Early programs were entered on punched cards — stiff cards roughly the size of a dollar bill, each holding 80 characters, one per column. A card reader shone light through the card: where a hole was punched light passed through, where it did not the light was blocked. That presence-or-absence is the physical basis of everything computers do. Underneath every file, image, and program is the binary number system: ones and zeros.

Precision

Each column held one character encoded as a specific combination of punches across the column's rows, not as a straight eight-bit byte. We will cover character encoding properly when we get to how data is represented.

What a large language model actually does

ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini are large language models. Stripped down, they predict the next word. If I say "Mary had a little ___," the overwhelmingly likely next word is lamb. Other words are possible but carry lower probability.

These models were trained by processing an enormous quantity of text. Your prompt goes into the model, and it emits the words with the highest probability of following. That simple mechanism turns out to have profound consequences — the model has read essentially everything written about, say, relativity, so it can produce a genuinely good explanation of it, and you can keep asking follow-up questions.

The catch

Models hallucinate. Because they are predicting plausible text rather than consulting a store of verified facts, they can produce confident, fluent, completely wrong answers. They have no internal sense of true or false. Verifying model output is one of the hardest open problems in the field — which is exactly why you have to verify it yourself.

Why GPUs power AI

A screen image is just numbers: every pixel has a red, green, and blue value from 0 to 255, and the combination produces the color you see. Critically, pixels at opposite corners of the screen are independent of each other, so their math can be done simultaneously. GPUs (graphics processing units) were built to do exactly that — enormous numbers of simple calculations in parallel.

It turned out that large language models are, underneath, huge matrices of numbers called weights, and running them requires the same kind of massively parallel arithmetic. So the hardware built for video games became the hardware that runs AI. One visible consequence: AI companies buying every high-end card available drove graphics card prices — and PC, laptop, and phone prices generally — sharply up.

Technology and jobs

The telephone was invented in the late 1800s. Early phones had no dial; you picked up and an operator connected your call, and telephone companies employed many thousands of them. Automation eliminated that job entirely. But it did not eliminate those workers — many retrained into new roles that had not existed before.

Economists call something like this a general purpose technology: an innovation broad enough to reshape the whole economy rather than one industry. The steam engine was one. Electricity, the computer, and the internet were others. AI appears to be the next. These technologies destroy categories of work and create new ones, and living through the transition means being the person who understands the tool rather than the person replaced by it.

Social engineering

Not every attack is technical. A caller phones a company's call center, chats pleasantly with an agent, and walks away with a name and a few details. A second caller then contacts that agent claiming to be from IT, uses those details to sound legitimate, reports a "problem with your system," and gets the agent to click a link. The system is compromised, and no software vulnerability was involved at any point. This is social engineering, and it is why security training matters as much as security software.

Related habits worth adopting now: keep your devices updated, run antivirus software, and be skeptical of offers that seem too good — including "best of" review articles that are actually paid advertisements for the product they rank first.
