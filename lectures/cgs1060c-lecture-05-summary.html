02The vocabulary, with a burglary analogy

TermDefinitionIn your neighborhood
ThreatSomeone out there who wants to cause harmBurglars exist in this city
VulnerabilityA weakness they could use — an unpatched system, a reused password, an unlocked doorYou left a window unlatched
ExploitThe act of taking advantage of that weaknessSomeone climbs through the window

Security vs. privacy

Security is about keeping unauthorized people out. Privacy is about controlling what happens to your data once you've legitimately shared it. Different problems, different defenses — a company can be perfectly secure and still sell everything it knows about you.

The CIA triad

Confidentiality — only the right people can see the data. Defenses: strong unique passwords, multi-factor authentication, encryption, HTTPS, VPNs.

Integrity — the data is correct and hasn't been tampered with or lost. Defenses: backups, versioning, keeping copies in more than one place.

Availability — you can get to it when you need it. Defenses: redundancy, a spare device, a UPS on your router, protection against denial-of-service attacks.

Not the Central Intelligence Agency.

Hats

  • White hat — an ethical hacker. Companies hire them for penetration tests, and many run bug bounty programs that pay researchers for reporting flaws. The term comes from Westerns: good guys wore white hats.
  • Black hat — attacking for criminal gain.
  • Script kiddie — someone with no real technical skill using tools (and increasingly AI) that others built. The lack of skill doesn't reduce the damage, or the legal consequences.
  • Hacktivism — attacking for a political or social cause. The motive may feel justified; it's still illegal.

Building a lab network at home to learn security is fine and encouraged. Touching systems you don't own is not, whatever the reason.

Security

03Malware, by type

Malware is any software running on your system that you didn't intend to be there. It arrives because you clicked something, or because a vulnerability let it in on its own.

TypeWhat makes it that type
VirusAttaches itself to a file or program and needs a person to run it. Then it copies itself into other files — the biological analogy is exact.
WormSpreads itself across a network with no user action at all. That self-propagation is the dividing line from a virus, not whether it carries a payload.
TrojanDisguised as something you want. Named for the wooden horse the Greeks left at Troy — the Trojans pulled it inside the walls themselves, and the soldiers hidden in it opened the gates that night.
RansomwareEncrypts your files with a key only the attacker has, then demands payment — usually cryptocurrency.
SpywareWatches quietly. Logs your keystrokes, records what sites you visit, captures passwords and PINs as you type them.
RootkitBuries itself so deep in the system that antivirus software may not detect it at all.
Botnet / zombieOne infected machine under remote control is a zombie. Thousands of them acting together are a botnet.

Distributed denial of service (DDoS)

A botnet — often built from cheap routers and other poorly-secured devices whose owners never notice — all requests the same website at once. The flood overwhelms the site and it goes down. Large organizations buy DDoS-protection services specifically to absorb this.

Ransomware, specifically

Ransomware actively hunts for everything it can reach, including attached backup drives and synced cloud folders. Two consequences:

  • Keep your backup drive physically disconnected except while a backup is running.
  • OneDrive version history is your escape hatch. Even if synced files get encrypted, you can restore them to a previous known-good state.

The FBI advises against paying. Even when attackers do provide a key — and they often do, because a reputation for not decrypting would end their business — your system is still infected, and you may simply be re-encrypted and asked for more. The most famous US case was the Colonial Pipeline attack in 2021, which disrupted fuel supply across the East Coast; the company paid roughly $4.4 million in bitcoin.

Macros in Office documents

Word and Excel can contain macros — small embedded programs. Useful, and also a classic malware delivery method. If a document warns you that macros were blocked, that is a stop sign. Don't enable them because the document itself tells you to.

"Enable Editing" is different and usually fine — that's Protected View, and you'll need to click it for the SimNet capstone file. The rule is about the source: enable editing on a file you deliberately downloaded from a site you trust; never on an unexpected attachment.

Fake infection pop-ups

A browser pop-up screaming that you're infected is almost always the attack, not a warning about one. Clicking it installs the thing it claims to be removing. Close the browser. Don't click anything inside the pop-up. If a machine is behaving strangely — searches redirecting to the wrong site, pop-ups appearing constantly — a reputable on-demand scanner such as Malwarebytes (free version, available for Windows and Mac) will scan and quarantine.

04Phishing and social engineering

Phishing — spelled with a ph — is a message designed to look legitimate so you'll click, log in somewhere fake, or hand over information.

VariantHow it arrives
PhishingA mass email — "your bank account needs attention"
Spear phishingTargeted at you, using your real name, employer, or recent activity
SmishingText message — a fake delivery notice, a fake bank alert
VishingPhone call or voicemail — someone claiming to be IT, your bank, or a relative
The old advice no longer works

Your textbook says you can spot phishing by its spelling and grammar mistakes. That is obsolete. AI writes clean, fluent, well-targeted messages. If anything, flawless English is now neutral evidence. You have to judge by the link and the ask, not the prose.

What to check instead

  • Hover over the link before clicking. Your mail client shows the real destination. One I received showed a friendly "claim your gift card" button pointing at password.land.
  • Read the domain right-to-left. microsoft.com.accountverify.co is not Microsoft — the real domain is the last part before the first slash, here accountverify.co.
  • Look for character substitution. rnicrosoft.com uses r followed by n, which at a glance reads as m.
  • Urgency and threats are a red flag. "Act now or your account closes."
  • Free things are a red flag. You very rarely get something for nothing.
  • A legitimate message tells you to go find them. "Call the number on our website" is a good sign; a link that does the work for you is not.

How to report it

Outlook at the college has a built-in Report button — use it and choose "phishing." That sends it to the information security team rather than just deleting it, which helps protect everyone else. Many employers run their own simulated phishing tests; failing one usually just means extra training, but the exercise exists because the real thing works.

Social engineering

The most effective attacks never touch a vulnerability. A caller befriends a call-centre agent and collects a name and extension. A second caller, armed with those details, claims to be from the help desk and talks the agent into visiting a website. The account is compromised, and no software flaw was involved.

AI voice cloning — tell your family about this one

It now takes very little recorded audio to generate a voice that sounds like a specific person. The scam targets older relatives: a call or voicemail that sounds exactly like a grandchild, stranded somewhere, needing money urgently.

Agree on a family code word. A phrase only your family knows, used to verify that an urgent call is real. Don't use your mother's maiden name — that's discoverable on Facebook and genealogy sites. A first pet works better.

Security

05Passwords, managers, and passkeys

The single most important habit

Never reuse a password. When some company gets breached, attackers take the email-and-password pairs and try them at banks, at Instagram, at everything. Reuse turns one company's bad day into your bad year.

Companies don't store your password directly — they store a hash of it. But hashes of common and predictable passwords can be reversed using precomputed dictionaries, which is why length and unpredictability matter more than swapping an o for a zero.

What NIST actually recommends

The National Institute of Standards and Technology sets US technical standards. Current guidance has moved away from what most of us were taught:

  • Long beats complex. A long passphrase you can remember is better than a short jumble you can't.
  • Forced 90-day expiry is no longer recommended — it pushes people toward predictable variations. Plenty of employers still require it anyway.
  • If it's so complex you write it down, it's counterproductive.

Password managers

The better answer: let software generate a long random password you never see or memorize.

  • Apple — iCloud Keychain, now the Passwords app
  • Google Password Manager — Chrome and Android system-wide
  • Microsoft Edge — built in
  • Third-party — 1Password and others, usually a subscription

Useful bonus: these managers check your saved passwords against known breach databases and flag the ones you need to change. Yes, you're trusting the vendor — but a reputable password manager is a far better bet than reusing one password everywhere.

Textbook correction: a password manager does not generate a new password at every login. It generates one strong password when you create or change the account, then remembers it.

Multi-factor authentication

MFA adds a second proof beyond the password — typically an approval on your phone. It defeats spyware that captured your typing, because your computer and your phone being compromised simultaneously is very unlikely. Turn it on everywhere that offers it, starting with your bank and your email.

Passkeys — where everything is heading

Why a passkey is stronger than any password

A passkey is a pair of cryptographic keys. The public key goes to the website; the private key stays in your password manager and never leaves your device.

Logging in, the site sends a random challenge. Your device solves it using the private key, proving who you are without transmitting any secret. There is no password to steal, phish, or leak in a breach — and because the key is bound to the real site's domain, a lookalike phishing page cannot trigger it.

In the demo: signing into GitHub produced a QR code, the phone scanned it, the two devices connected over Bluetooth, a fingerprint unlocked the passkey, and the login completed with no password typed. Because the passkey lives in the password manager rather than the handset, replacing a lost phone restores it.

Protect your phone PIN in public

If someone learns your PIN and then steals your phone, they have your email, your password manager, and your MFA approvals — your whole digital life. Thieves work in pairs: one watches you type, the other takes the phone.

Unlock with your face or fingerprint in public. Save the PIN for private places. And if a stranger asks you to take their photo, don't hand over an unlocked phone.

Security

06Antivirus, firewalls, and patching

Antivirus — and its real limitation

Microsoft Defender is built into Windows, on by default, and genuinely good. macOS has a built-in equivalent called XProtect that Apple doesn't surface in the interface. Don't run two antivirus products at once — they interfere with each other.

Traditional antivirus works from signature files — fingerprints of known malware. Before a program runs, it's checked against that list. The limitation follows directly: a brand-new threat has no signature yet. That's why keeping signatures updated is not optional. Windows will warn you when they go stale.

A firewall is not antivirus

Your home router already acts as a firewall. Devices inside your house hold private IP addresses that nobody on the internet can reach directly — only your router is visible from outside. Unsolicited inbound connections are blocked by default.

What it doesn't do is stop outbound connections. If malware is already running on your machine, it can freely call out to its command-and-control server. A firewall is the guard at the door, not a search of everyone already inside.

Sign to watch for: your router showing connections to systems in places you've never dealt with.

Patching, and where fixes come from

A researcher — usually a white hat — finds a flaw and reports it privately to the vendor, sometimes for a bug bounty. The vendor develops a fix, publishes it, and only then issues an advisory describing the flaw. Microsoft concentrates its security updates on Patch Tuesday, the second Tuesday of each month.

Advisories carry severity scores. The worst allow an unauthenticated user to execute arbitrary code with root privileges — an attacker running whatever they like, as the most powerful account on the system.

All of which reduces to one instruction: turn on automatic updates, and restart when asked.

HTTPS and certificates

HTTPS encrypts traffic between your browser and the site, and also proves the site is who it claims. Click the padlock to inspect the certificate: the domain it was issued for, the organization, the certificate authority that signed it, and the validity dates. Your browser ships with a list of authorities it trusts — a chain of trust.

Certificates expire, which is why your computer's clock must be correct; a badly wrong clock makes valid certificates appear invalid. And if you ever get a certificate warning, don't click through it.

Physical and environmental

Hardware fails, and South Florida adds hurricanes, lightning, and power loss. Surge protectors on anything valuable; a small UPS on the router and modem keeps you online through a short outage. Availability is part of security.

Privacy

07Cookies, trackers, and data brokers

A cookie is a small piece of named data a website stores in your browser and receives back on every subsequent visit. You can see them yourself: right-click a page → Inspect → Application → Cookies. Some expire when you close the tab (session cookies); others persist for years.

TypeSet byPurpose
First-partyThe site you're actually visitingKeeping you logged in, remembering your cart and preferences. Mostly benign and often necessary.
Third-partyAn ad or tracker embedded in that pageFollowing you between sites to build an advertising profile.

Firefox blocks third-party cookies by default. Chrome does not — if you want them blocked, go into Settings and turn it on, along with the "do not track" request.

Why blocking cookies didn't end tracking

Tracking moved. The Meta Pixel is a small piece of JavaScript that sites embed voluntarily, reporting your visit back to Meta as first-party code — collecting much of what third-party cookies used to. This is how advertisers target by age, location, sex, and inferred interest, and why a product you looked at on Amazon follows you into a streaming show.

Data brokers

Data brokers buy, combine, and resell personal information. Subscription services exist (roughly $10–20/month, often with family plans) that send deletion requests to hundreds of brokers on your behalf, every month. The legal basis traces back to GDPR and its right to erasure, which many companies now honor globally rather than maintaining two systems.

Private browsing — what it does and doesn't do

Incognito or private mode stops your browser from keeping history and cookies after you close the window. It does not hide your IP address, and the site still knows you visited. For that you'd need a VPN, which hides your traffic from the local network and your ISP, and shows the site the VPN's address instead of yours.

Fake Wi-Fi hotspots

Anyone can broadcast a network name. At a conference, a café, or on campus, confirm the real network name before connecting — "MDC Guest," not something that merely looks close. A fake access point exists to watch what passes through it.

Check your own exposure

haveibeenpwned.com (spelled "pwned") tells you which breaches have included your email address. Worth doing for every address you use. My college address turned up in a McGraw-Hill breach — the publisher of this course's textbooks — which is a useful reminder that this isn't theoretical. Since I don't reuse passwords, the practical risk is spam rather than account takeover. That's the whole argument for not reusing passwords, in one example.

Security

08Your actual checklist

  • Turn on automatic updates on every device, and restart when prompted.
  • Confirm your antivirus and firewall are on. Windows: Windows Security. Mac: System Settings → Privacy & Security.
  • Stop reusing passwords. Start a password manager — you already have one built into your phone and browser.
  • Turn on MFA for email, banking, and school accounts.
  • Try a passkey somewhere that offers one.
  • Check your email at haveibeenpwned.com and change anything flagged.
  • Back up to two places, and keep the external drive unplugged between backups.
  • Agree a family code word against voice-cloning scams, and tell older relatives why.
  • Hover before you click. Report phishing rather than just deleting it.
  • Unlock your phone biometrically in public, never by typing the PIN.

Textbook drift worth knowing: Safe Mode is no longer F8 — hold Shift while clicking Restart.

Microsoft Excel

09Chapter 3: functions

How SimNet expects you to enter functions

There are three ways: the Function Arguments dialog, autocomplete as you type, or selecting cells with the mouse. Most SimNet tasks are built around the Function Arguments dialog, so practice that route even if typing is faster for you.

SUM and ranges

=SUM(A1,A2,A3) lists individual cells. =SUM(A1:A10) uses a range — the colon means "through." Prefer ranges: add a value inside the range later and the total updates by itself.

AVERAGE and the blank-cell trap

This one shows up on assessments

AVERAGE ignores blank cells. It does not ignore zeros.

Three cells containing 30, 60, 90 average to 60. Replace one with an empty cell and the remaining two average to 90 — the blank isn't counted at all. Replace it with a zero and you get 60, because zero is a number. A blank and a zero look almost identical in a spreadsheet and produce completely different answers.

The COUNT family

FunctionCounts
COUNTCells containing numbers
COUNTACells that are not empty (text included)
COUNTBLANKCells that are empty

Date and time functions

=TODAY() returns today's date. =NOW() returns date and time. Recall that Excel stores a date as the number of days since January 1, 1900 — so with NOW(), the whole number is the date and the decimal fraction is the time of day. Format as a plain number to see it.

Custom date formats use codes: MM month, DD day, YYYY year, HH hour, MM minute (in a time context), SS second.

TODAY() and NOW() recalculate every time the file opens

Useful for a dashboard that should always show the current date. Wrong for a letter template — the date would change every time anyone opened it. For a date that stays put, type it, or use Ctrl + ;, which inserts today's date as a fixed value.

Text functions

FunctionDoes
PROPERCapitalizes like a name — maria lopez → Maria Lopez
UPPER / LOWERAll caps / all lowercase
LENCounts characters — spaces count
CONCATENATEJoins text. Adds no space — you must supply " " yourself
TEXTJOINJoins a whole range with a separator you choose, and takes an ignore_empty argument: TRUE skips blanks, FALSE leaves doubled separators

Referencing other sheets and workbooks

Start a formula with =, click a different sheet tab, click the cell you want. The reference updates automatically when the source changes. You can also reference a cell in a different file using bracket notation — Excel stores the file's path, so the link survives as long as you don't move or rename the source.

Watch for circular references: if A2 depends on B2 and B2 depends on A2, Excel can't resolve it and will tell you so.

You can also name a cell or range — right-click → Define Name — and then use that name in formulas. =price*quantity is far easier to read and debug than =B4*C4.

PMT — the most useful function in this chapter

=PMT(rate, nper, pv) calculates a loan payment.

The trap: all three arguments must use the same time unit. Loans are advertised as an annual rate over a number of years, but you pay monthly. So divide the rate by 12 and multiply the years by 12:

=PMT(annual_rate/12, years*12, price)

A $25,000 car at 6% over 5 years gives about $483.32 a month. The result comes out negative because it's money leaving you; put a minus sign in front of PMT to display it positive.

Once it's built, change one cell to see what a different rate or term does. A $250,000 mortgage at 7.5% over 30 years runs roughly $1,700 a month before taxes and insurance — and over the full term you pay more in interest than the house cost.

Microsoft Excel

10Lookups, logic, and errors

IF

=IF(test, value_if_true, value_if_false). Comparison operators: =, <> (not equal), <, >, <=, >=.

VLOOKUP

=VLOOKUP(lookup_value, table_range, column_number, FALSE) — search the first column of the range for a value, and return whatever sits in the numbered column of the matching row.

Always pass FALSE

The last argument controls match type. FALSE means exact match. Leave it out and Excel does an approximate match, which requires the first column to be sorted ascending and otherwise returns wrong answers with no error at all. Silent wrongness is worse than an error message — always pass FALSE unless you specifically want a range lookup.

XLOOKUP — the better option where available

=XLOOKUP(lookup_value, lookup_array, return_array, "not found"). You give the column to search and the column to return directly, instead of counting column positions, so inserting a column doesn't break it. It defaults to exact match, and the fourth argument lets you supply your own message instead of #N/A.

Error values

ErrorMeans
#####Not an error — the column is too narrow. Double-click the column border.
#VALUE!Wrong type of data — usually arithmetic on text
#NAME?Excel doesn't recognize a name — a typo'd function or an undefined named range
#DIV/0!Division by zero, often because the divisor cell is still empty
#N/AA lookup found no match

One more: don't type commas inside numbers. Entering 1,000 may be stored as text and break your formulas. Type 1000 and let formatting add the comma for display.

Microsoft Excel

11Chapter 4: printing a wide spreadsheet

Spreadsheets are wider than paper, so Excel splits them across pages in whatever way it likes unless you intervene. In File → Print you can see the result before committing:

  • Orientation → Landscape — more columns per page, the first thing to try.
  • Scaling → Fit All Columns on One Page — forces the width to fit, shrinking the text.
  • Page Layout view shows page boundaries while you edit, so you can see what will break where.

To produce a PDF instead of paper, choose Save as PDF in the print dialog (Mac: the PDF dropdown at the bottom left; Windows: select Microsoft Print to PDF as the printer).
