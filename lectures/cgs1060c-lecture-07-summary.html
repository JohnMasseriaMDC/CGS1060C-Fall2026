AI tools

01NotebookLM in practice

Quick recap from last week. A plain chatbot answers from its training — and by the time billions of documents are compressed into a model's parameters, every trace of where a fact came from is gone. That's why models hallucinate, and why they usually can't cite anything.

RAG — Retrieval Augmented Generation — fixes this by searching for relevant documents first and feeding their actual text to the model along with your question. NotebookLM is RAG where you choose the documents. No web search; it uses only what you upload.

Getting started

  1. Go to notebook.google.com and sign in — your MDC Google account works.
  2. Click Create new notebook.
  3. Upload your sources (PDFs, and it also accepts other formats including video).
  4. Ask questions in the chat panel.
Watch the footnotes

Every answer carries small numbered links. Click them. They jump to the exact passage in the source document that the answer came from, so you can confirm the tool read it correctly.

This is the whole value of NotebookLM over a general chatbot, and it's the habit to build: trust but verify. Grounding makes hallucination far less likely — it doesn't make it impossible, particularly when sources conflict or say something ambiguous.

What it does when it doesn't know

In the demo I deliberately asked something the documents don't answer — what the spa's most expensive service is. The response: "The sources do not state what Bayside Day Spa's most expensive service is, as they do not include a complete price menu." Then it offered what the sources do say.

I didn't have to instruct it not to make something up. Being restricted to the sources is the default behavior, and that's exactly why this tool suits studying.

Other things it can do

FeatureWhat it produces
Mind mapA visual breakdown of the topics across your sources. Downloads as a PNG you can drop into a document.
Audio overviewA spoken summary, podcast-style. Takes a minute or two to generate — it writes the script first, then runs it through text-to-speech.

You could upload textbook chapters to generate practice questions. If you do, keep that notebook private — the textbook is copyrighted material and sharing it publicly isn't yours to do.

Computer hardware

03What's in the box

Hardware vs. software

Hardware is what you can physically touch. Software is the instructions that tell it what to do.

The old analogy still works: your record player was the hardware, the record was the software. Same machine, different music.

ComponentRole
CPURuns the instructions — the general-purpose brain
RAMWorking memory. Volatile — contents vanish when power is lost
SSD / hard drivePermanent storage. Non-volatile — survives power loss
GPUMassively parallel math; graphics, video, and AI
MotherboardThe board everything plugs into; carries the buses that connect them
InputKeyboard, trackpad, camera, microphone, touchscreen
OutputDisplay, speakers, printer

Why sleep is instant and a cold boot isn't

Close your laptop lid and it enters a very low power state — just enough electricity to keep the bits alive in volatile RAM. Everything is still there, so opening the lid resumes immediately.

A full power-off empties RAM completely. On restart the machine must run self-tests and reload the operating system from the SSD, which takes real time.

Practical consequence: work you haven't saved lives only in volatile RAM. Lose power and it's gone. That's the whole argument for AutoSave.

Categories of computer

Desktops, laptops, 2-in-1s that fold into tablets, tablets with keyboards, smartphones — all personal devices, one user at a time. Then embedded computers, which are everywhere and invisible: your smart TV, your thermostat, your watch, the little board inside a piece of lab equipment. And shared machines — servers, mainframes, and the clustered data centers running AI models.

Computer hardware

04The processor, clock speed, and heat

The fetch–decode–execute cycle

A CPU does the same four things endlessly: fetch an instruction from memory, decode what it means, execute it (add, multiply, compare), store the result. Then fetch the next one. Billions of times a second.

What gigahertz measures

A hertz is one cycle per second. A megahertz is a million (FM radio broadcasts in this range). A gigahertz is a billion. A chip running at 3.8 GHz has an internal clock ticking 3.8 billion times per second, and most modern CPUs run between 2 and 5 GHz.

A textbook nuance

Your book may describe gigahertz as "billions of instructions per second." Strictly it's billions of clock cycles per second. Only if a chip completes one instruction per cycle do those numbers match — and modern chips sometimes do several, sometimes need several cycles for one.

Cores and hyperthreading

Early CPUs had one core and did one thing at a time. Modern ones have 4, 8, or 16; high-end desktops reach 32; large servers go to 192. Hyperthreading lets a single core work on two instruction streams at once, taking advantage of the idle moments while the core waits on memory.

Faster clock = more heat. This is the fundamental limit.

Every switching transistor gives off heat, and heat is what stops chips going faster.

Heat sink — a metal block with large surface area, pressed against the chip, drawing heat away. Fan — moves cooler air across the heat sink's fins. Liquid cooling — used in high-end machines, because liquids carry away far more heat than air can.

You know this from experience: 60°F air is cool but survivable, while 60°F water will pull the heat out of you dangerously fast.

Chips also carry thermal sensors. Too hot and the system will slow the clock down — thermal throttling — or shut off entirely rather than damage itself. That's why a laptop with blocked vents gets slow.

05CPU vs. GPU vs. TPU vs. NPU

From the video. These aren't competitors — they're a division of labor.

ChipStands forWhat it's for
CPUCentral Processing UnitGeneral-purpose work: the operating system, applications, logic, anything sequential. Few cores, each very capable.
GPUGraphics Processing UnitMassive parallel math. Thousands of simple cores. Games, video, and training large AI models.
TPUTensor Processing UnitGoogle's custom AI chip, built for data centers — their answer to depending on GPU vendors.
NPUNeural Processing UnitSmall, power-efficient AI chip inside your phone or laptop. Runs already-trained models locally (inference), not training.
Why a GPU beats a CPU at graphics and AI

Think about the pixels on your screen. What happens in one corner is independent of the other corner, so those calculations can all happen simultaneously.

A CPU with 8 powerful cores does 8 things at once. A GPU has thousands of much simpler cores — they only add, multiply, and divide, but there are enormous numbers of them. For a million independent calculations, that wins overwhelmingly.

And it turns out a neural network is, mathematically, stacks of matrices, and running one is mostly matrix multiplication — the same parallel problem. Hardware built for gamers became the hardware that runs AI.

The NPU is why your phone can do AI offline. Apple calls theirs the Neural Engine; Qualcomm builds it into Snapdragon; Intel and AMD now ship laptop chips with one. Background blur on a video call, voice isolation, making a dark photo usable, live transcription — that's the NPU, and it's faster and more private because nothing leaves the device.

Instruction sets: x86 vs. ARM

Chips speak different machine languages. x86 is Intel's, and AMD uses the same one — so software compiled for x86 runs on both. ARM is a different instruction set, used by Apple's M-series and Qualcomm's Snapdragon.

It's like English and Spanish. Both express the same ideas; a book must be published in one or the other. ARM came from the RISC approach — Reduced Instruction Set Computer — a simpler set of instructions that could run very efficiently. Windows was x86-only for years; Microsoft has since ported it to ARM.

Who makes GPUs

NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel all do; NVIDIA currently makes the most powerful. Their market went through three waves: gaming, then cryptocurrency mining, and now AI. Each wave made GPUs harder for ordinary buyers to get.

06The memory hierarchy

One idea explains the whole ladder: distance costs time

Electrical signals travel at close to the speed of light — fast, but not instant. The further data has to travel, the longer it takes. So computers are built as a hierarchy: tiny and fast closest to the processor, huge and slow furthest away.

LevelWhereSpeed
RegistersInside the CPU core — where arithmetic actually happensFastest. Only a handful exist.
L1 cacheOn the chip, closest to the coreExtremely fast, very small
L2 / L3 cacheOn the chip, progressively further outFast, progressively larger
RAMSeparate chips on the motherboardSlower — the signal leaves the processor
SSDStorage inside the machineAbout 1 millisecond
Hard driveSpinning storage10–100 ms when busy
Network / internetAnother computer entirelySlowest by far

How cache works

When the CPU needs an instruction, it checks the cache first. If it's there — a hit — it's retrieved almost instantly. If not, it goes out to RAM, and while it's there it grabs a large block rather than one item, on the reasonable bet that what you need next sits nearby. When cache fills up, the least recently used contents get overwritten.

Launching Chrome for the first time shows the whole ladder in action: the program loads from SSD into RAM, instructions move from RAM into cache, and from cache into registers to execute. Each step is faster and smaller than the one before.

There is a global RAM shortage — and it's why computers cost more

AI data centers are consuming enormous quantities of memory, and chip manufacturers are redirecting production toward the faster, more expensive memory those data centers want. That leaves less being made for ordinary laptops and phones. Same dynamic as generic drug shortages: manufacturers favor the higher-margin product.

It also affects SSDs and SD cards. And it won't resolve quickly — a new fabrication plant takes two to three years to build.

Buying advice: 16 GB of RAM is a realistic minimum now, not the 4–16 GB range the textbook quotes. And on most modern laptops — all Macs, increasingly everything — memory is soldered in and cannot be upgraded later. Buy more than you need today.

Computer hardware

07Storage: spinning disks vs. solid state

Inside a hard drive

Spinning metal platters — 7,200 RPM in a laptop, up to 15,000 in a server — with data written in concentric circles, and a read-write head on a moving arm. Moving that arm to a different track is a seek, and it takes milliseconds. That mechanical movement is the entire performance problem.

Thrashing is what happens when several programs want data from opposite ends of the disk at once: the arm spends its time travelling rather than reading. It's why older machines bogged down with several applications open.

Solid-state drives

No moving parts — the same flash technology as the SD card in a camera. Truly random access: reaching any byte costs the same as reaching any other. Faster, quieter, more shock-resistant, and lower power.

Why spinning disks still exist

Cost per byte at very high capacity. Backup systems write large files start-to-finish, which is sequential — the weakness of a spinning disk never shows up. For databases and anything doing scattered reads, SSDs win by an enormous margin.

Textbook drift: SSDs don't need defragmenting, and modern operating systems optimize them automatically.

08Bits, bytes, and character encoding

Everything in a computer is zeros and ones. Eight bits make a byte, and a byte holds 256 distinct values.

ASCII

ASCII — American Standard Code for Information Interchange — assigns a number to each English character. It defines 128 characters, which fits comfortably in a byte with room to spare.

CharacterASCII value
The digit 048
Uppercase A65
Lowercase a97

A consequence worth knowing: uppercase letters have lower values than lowercase, so a sort by raw character code puts ZEBRA before apple. That's why a name typed in all caps sometimes jumps to the top of a sorted list.

Unicode

ASCII covers English and not much else — no Spanish ñ, no Cyrillic, no Greek, no Hebrew or Arabic, and certainly not the thousands of Chinese characters. Unicode solves this with a scheme covering roughly 150,000 characters, still growing — and most new additions now are emoji.

Textbook drift: the book says Unicode characters take 2 bytes. That's one encoding (UTF-16). The dominant one today, UTF-8, uses 1 to 4 bytes depending on the character — so English text stays one byte per character and only other scripts cost more.

Other number systems exist too — binary (digits 0–1), octal (0–7), and hexadecimal. Fun detail: "10" exists in every system but means something different in each. In binary it's 2, in octal it's 8, in decimal it's 10.

Correction from class

I said HDMI carries content encryption and DisplayPort doesn't. Both support HDCP, the copy-protection scheme used by streaming devices. The real differences lie elsewhere: HDMI carries audio-return and TV remote-control features, while DisplayPort supports daisy-chaining several monitors from one port.

Why Ethernet cables are twisted

Open one and you find eight wires in four twisted pairs. Any wire carrying current acts a little like an antenna — radiating interference, and picking it up. Twisting the pairs makes the two wires' fields cancel, which both protects the signal and keeps it from disturbing its neighbors.

StandardRangeUsed for

Wi-Fi and Bluetooth share the 2.4 GHz band — the same frequency a microwave oven uses. A leaking microwave can genuinely disrupt Wi-Fi nearby, and if yours is leaking that's a reason to replace it for more than network reasons.

Bluetooth's short range is also why the passkey demo worked the way it did: your phone and laptop pair over Bluetooth to prove they're physically in the same room.

10Display resolution — the question from class

Standard HD is 1920 × 1080 pixels. 4K is 3840 × 2160.

4K has four times the pixels, not twice

You doubled the width and you doubled the height. 2 × 2 = 4.

1920 × 1080 ≈ 2.1 million pixels. 3840 × 2160 ≈ 8.3 million. The name makes it sound like twice as much; it's four times. And going from 4K to 8K quadruples it again.

This is why 4K streaming demands so much more bandwidth. Compression is what makes it practical — and much of that compression work runs on the GPU.

It also explains something you've probably seen: watch a football game and the image goes blocky during fast action. A talking head barely changes between frames, so there's little new information to send. Twenty-two players moving at once means almost every pixel changes, the compressor can't keep up, and you see pixelation.

Computer hardware

11What happens when you press the power button

  1. Firmware runs. The BIOS — Basic Input Output System, now usually called UEFI — lives in read-only, non-volatile memory on the motherboard. It's deliberately write-protected, because malware in your firmware would survive any reinstall and could leave the machine permanently unusable, or bricked.
  2. Self-tests. Memory and essential hardware are checked. This is what the manufacturer logo is hiding.
  3. The TPM verifies integrity. The Trusted Platform Module confirms the firmware and operating system haven't been tampered with and that the hardware hasn't changed.
  4. Keys are released. If the checks pass, the TPM hands over the disk encryption key so the OS can read your encrypted drive.
  5. The operating system loads from SSD into RAM, and you get a desktop.

Your phone does the same sequence — which is why a full restart takes noticeably longer than waking from sleep.
