Networking

02How data actually moves

The old way: circuit switching

On a traditional telephone network, dialing a number physically connected your line to the other phone through switches at the central office. For the length of the call you had a dedicated circuit — real copper, end to end. It worked, but the circuit was yours alone whether you were talking or not, and if any part of the path failed, the call dropped.

The new way: packet switching

The internet grew out of US Department of Defense research in the 1950s and 60s. The problem they were solving was blunt: build a communications network that keeps working when parts of it are destroyed. ARPANET — from the Advanced Research Projects Agency — was the direct ancestor, with its first links in 1969.

The answer was to stop reserving circuits. Instead, break every message into small packets and send them independently.

What's inside a packet
  • A destination address — where it's going
  • A sequence number — "I'm packet 3 of 7," so the receiver can reassemble them in the right order even if they arrive jumbled
  • A checksum — a value that lets the receiver verify that no bits were corrupted in transit. Bad checksum means the packet is discarded and retransmitted.

Each node in the network connects to several others, so if one route fails the packets simply go around. The internet has millions of these nodes, which is where its resilience comes from. Think of it as a network of networks — Miami Dade College runs its own network across campuses and also connects outward to everyone else's.

Why your call stutters but your music recovers

Drive away from a cell tower and the signal weakens, so packets get lost. What happens next depends on the application:

  • Streaming music can wait. Lost packets get retransmitted, the audio pauses, then resumes where it left off.
  • A live voice call can't wait — a delayed word is worse than a missing one. So lost packets are simply dropped, which is why you hear gaps or every other word.

The internet's core has enormous capacity and redundancy. Problems almost always happen in the last mile — the connection between you and your provider.

03Bandwidth, latency, and satellites

Two different things

Bandwidth is how much data you can move per second — measured in megabits per second (Mbps). Think of it as how wide the road is.

Latency is how long it takes a single message to get there and back — measured in milliseconds (ms). Think of it as how long the drive is. Driving 300 miles to Gainesville at 50 mph gives you a latency of six hours, no matter how many lanes the highway has.

Data travels close to the speed of light — about 186,000 miles per second, or 3 × 10⁸ meters per second. Fast, but not instant. Miami to Northern Virginia and back lands around 20–30 ms, which you'd never notice.

Why satellite internet used to be unusable

Traditional satellites sit in geostationary orbit, roughly 22,000 miles up. A signal must climb all the way there and come back down, which costs 500–700 ms round trip. That's long enough to see: type a character, wait, watch it appear. (Shipboard systems at sea ran exactly like this.)

Starlink changed the arithmetic by putting many small satellites in low Earth orbit, a few hundred miles up instead of twenty-two thousand. The distance collapses, so the latency does too — modern satellite internet now feels essentially normal.

Bits and bytes

A bit is a single binary value: 0 or 1. Eight bits make a byte, which has 256 possible values — enough for every uppercase letter, lowercase letter, digit, and punctuation mark. Network speeds are quoted in bits per second; file sizes are in bytes. A 300-baud modem moved 300 bits per second, which is about 30 characters a second — text only, one line at a time.

Correction from class

I said in class that Wi-Fi stands for "Wireless Fidelity." It doesn't stand for anything — it's a brand name created by a marketing agency for the Wi-Fi Alliance, and "Wireless Fidelity" was a tagline attached briefly afterward and then dropped. The textbook may repeat the myth; it's still a myth.

Cable types

  • Coaxial — a copper core, a plastic insulator (the dielectric), and a metal shield. The shield blocks interference, which is what lets coax carry a lot of data with little signal loss. This is typical cable-internet wiring.
  • Fiber optic — glass, carrying light rather than electricity. Immune to moisture and electrical interference, which matters near the coast where salt air degrades copper. The trade-off: it's physically glass, so a sharp bend can break it, and repair needs specialist tools.
  • Twisted-pair Ethernet — the familiar copper patch cable with the clip.
Practical: call 811 before you dig

Those small orange marker posts along roadsides flag buried fiber. Before digging on your own property, call 811 — a free national service that sends someone to spray-paint where the buried electrical, cable, and fiber lines run. Hitting a buried electrical line can kill you; hitting fiber is merely expensive.

A home network, end to end

Your laptop connects by Wi-Fi or Ethernet to a router (often built into the cable modem). The modem connects by coax or fiber to your ISP. The ISP connects to the internet backbone. The backbone carries your packets through many routers to the web server you asked for, which has its own routers in front of it. Dozens of hops, every time, in milliseconds.

05IP addresses, MAC addresses, DHCP, and DNS

An IP address is the postal address of a device on a network. Give a map app a street address and it can route a car there; give the internet an IP address and it can route packets there.

Public vs. private

Your public IP address is what the rest of the internet sees. Everyone on your home network — or everyone in a large company office — typically shares a single public address. That's one of the tricks keeping IPv4 alive.

Your private IP address is the one your device holds inside the local network. Ranges beginning 192.168. and 10. are reserved for private use, which is why nearly every home router hands out something like 192.168.1.x. Your router translates between the two as traffic goes in and out.

MAC address

A MAC address is a hardware address burned into the network adapter by its manufacturer. It identifies the physical interface, not its position on the network. Within a local network, your device translates a local IP address into the MAC address it needs to actually deliver the data.

The distinction that shows up on quizzes

MAC address — set by the manufacturer, burned into the hardware, permanent.
Private IP address — handed out by your router, can change.
Public IP address — assigned by your ISP, shared across your network.

DHCP and DNS

DHCP — Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol — is how your router hands each device an IP address automatically. Before DHCP, an administrator had to walk to every machine and assign addresses by hand.

DNS — the Domain Name System — converts names into addresses. You type mdc.edu; DNS returns the numeric IP address your computer actually needs. (In class I called it "Dynamic Name Service." Domain Name System is correct.)

The one diagnostic worth memorizing

Run ipconfig and if your address starts with 169.254., your computer failed to get an address from the router and assigned itself a placeholder. That is not a working connection.

On Ethernet: check the link lights on the switch port, try a different port, try a different cable.
On Wi-Fi: you probably aren't actually connected to the network.

If pages won't load but your connection otherwise looks fine, suspect DNS. Chrome will usually say outright that it couldn't resolve the name.

In the live traceroute demo, each hop came back with a name as well as an address — Griffin, Pompano, North Dade. Providers name routers after real places so technicians can locate a fault geographically. Note also that the trace from the corporate network died immediately: many networks deliberately block the packets traceroute depends on.

07HTTP, HTTPS, and encryption

Tim Berners-Lee, working at CERN in Switzerland, invented the World Wide Web between 1989 and 1991. Three of his inventions are still the foundation of everything you do online:

  • HTML — HyperText Markup Language, the code that describes a web page. Right-click any page and choose View page source to see it.
  • HTTP — HyperText Transfer Protocol, the rules for requesting and delivering those pages.
  • URL — Uniform Resource Locator, the address of a page.
What the S adds

HTTPS encrypts everything between your browser and the server. Someone intercepting the traffic sees only meaningless data, because only your browser and that server hold the key.

The keys are typically 256 bits. That's about 1.2 × 10⁷⁷ possible values — a number in the same territory as the count of atoms in the observable universe. Simply counting that high would take more energy than the Sun will produce in its entire lifetime. Brute force is not a realistic attack.

Check for the lock or security indicator in the address bar. Most major sites now redirect you from HTTP to HTTPS automatically, and browsers warn loudly when a site isn't secured.

The quantum caveat

A sufficiently large quantum computer could break today's encryption — and that drives a real strategy called harvest now, decrypt later: capture encrypted traffic today and store it until the hardware exists. Practical quantum attacks are likely still years away, and post-quantum encryption algorithms designed to resist them already exist. We'll return to this in the emerging-technologies unit.

Microsoft Excel

09Chapter 1: what's really in a cell

The idea everything else depends on

Every cell has two layers: what it displays, and what it contains. The cell might show $1,250.00 while actually containing the number 1250. It might show 30 while containing =10+20. It might show 9/30/26 while containing 46295.

The formula bar always shows what's really in there. When Excel surprises you, look at the formula bar first.

Cell addresses and the status bar

A cell's address is its column letter plus its row number — A1, B7, D23. The bottom-left of the window shows the current mode: Ready (a cell is selected), Enter (you're typing new content), or Edit (you're modifying existing content).

The trap that will cost you points

The $ button on the ribbon does not apply Currency format. It applies Accounting format. Hover over it and Excel says so: "Accounting Number Format."

When SimNet or the exam tells you to apply currency formatting, clicking that button marks you wrong. Instead use the number-format dropdown and choose Currency, or right-click → Format Cells → Currency.

Visual tell: Currency puts the dollar sign right against the number. Accounting pins it to the far left of the cell.

The small increase/decrease decimal buttons change how many decimal places display — they don't change the stored value.

Microsoft Excel

10Dates and autofill

Dates are just numbers

Excel stores a date as the number of days since January 1, 1900, which is serial number 1. Type 46295, format the cell as a date, and you get 9/30/26. Format it back to General and the number returns. (In class I first said 1970 and corrected it later — 1900 is right.)

This has a genuinely useful consequence: you can do arithmetic on dates. =A1+1 gives you tomorrow. Subtract two dates and you get the number of days between them.

Format dates as YYYY-MM-DD when the data will be reused

Right-click → Format Cells → Date gives you many display options. The year-month-day form has a practical advantage: it sorts correctly as text in any other program you export to. Also use four-digit years whenever your data includes dates from the last century — birthdates, for instance.

Shortcut: Ctrl + ; inserts today's date into the current cell. It works the same on Mac — that's the Control key, not Command.

A piece of leap-year trivia that's actually relevant

A year is a leap year if it divides evenly by 4 — except century years, which must divide by 400. So 1900 was not a leap year, while 2000 was. All of this exists because Earth's orbit takes roughly 365¼ days, not 365.

The wrinkle: Excel deliberately treats 1900 as a leap year anyway, to stay compatible with Lotus 1-2-3, which had the bug first. It's a rare case of software preserving a known error on purpose.

11Formulas and references

Every formula starts with =. Without it, Excel stores what you typed as text — 123+321 just sits there as characters.

Reference cells rather than typing values. =A1+A2 recalculates automatically whenever A1 or A2 changes. That automatic recalculation is the entire point of a spreadsheet.

Don't hard-code values you might change

=A1*7.5% works until the tax rate changes, and then you edit every formula by hand. =A1*B1 with the rate in B1 means you change one cell. Both produce the same answer today; only one survives next year.

Relative vs. absolute references

Copy a formula down a column and Excel adjusts the row numbers automatically. =B2*C2 becomes =B3*C3, then =B4*C4. That's a relative reference, and it's usually exactly what you want — Excel is really storing "two cells to my left times one cell to my left."

But sometimes a formula must always point at one specific cell — a tax rate in B1, say. Copy =A2*B1 down and it becomes =A3*B2, then =A4*B3, pointing at empty cells and eventually producing #VALUE!.

The fix: lock it with dollar signs

$B$1 — column and row both locked. Never changes when copied.
B$1 — row locked only.
$B1 — column locked only.
B1 — fully relative.

Here the dollar sign means "absolute reference." It has nothing to do with currency — a confusing overlap, but a different context entirely.

You can also give a cell a name and use that name in formulas, which is self-documenting and never shifts.

Order of operations

School taught PEMDAS: parentheses, exponents, multiplication/division, addition/subtraction. Excel's order is slightly longer:

  1. Parentheses
  2. Negation (making a number negative)
  3. Percent
  4. Exponentiation
  5. Multiplication and division, left to right
  6. Addition and subtraction, left to right

So =2+3*4 is 14, not 20. And note the consequence of negation ranking above exponentiation: =-2^2 returns positive 4 in Excel, where standard math notation would give −4.

Functions

=SUM(A1,A2,A3) adds the listed cells. =SUM(A1:A10) adds everything in the range — the colon means "through." Ranges are better: add a value inside the range and the total updates by itself.

Chart vocabulary

Terms you'll be asked to identify: the chart title, the x-axis and y-axis with their titles, the legend, the plot area, and a data series — one set of related values, usually a row or column from your sheet.

Original cellRelative references
Copy and pasteStays as it wasAdjust to the new position

Paste Special

A normal paste brings values, formulas, and formatting together. Paste Special lets you take only part of that — most usefully Values, which pastes the calculated result and discards the formula. The pasted number is then frozen: change the source cells and it won't update. That's sometimes exactly what you want.

Column widths

##### is not an error. It means the column is too narrow to display the value. Fixes:

  • Double-click the boundary between column headers to auto-fit to the widest entry.
  • Drag the boundary to set a width by hand — Excel shows the measurement as you drag.
  • Select several columns first, then drag one, to make them all the same width.

Delete vs. Delete

Pressing Delete clears a cell's contents and leaves the empty cell in place. Right-click → Delete removes the cell, row, or column entirely and shifts the surrounding cells up or left. Insert does the reverse. Choose deliberately — the two have very different effects on everything below.

Conditional formatting

Change a cell's appearance based on its value: select the range, choose Conditional Formatting, pick a rule such as Highlight Cells Rules → Greater Than, enter a threshold, and choose a fill. Cells that meet the condition are formatted automatically, and they update as values change.
